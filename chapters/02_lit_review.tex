\section*{The Biological Basis of Gene Regulation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Biological Basis of Gene Expression and Regulation}
\subsection*{Gene Expression and Transcription Profiling}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Gene Expression and Transcription Profiling}

Genes code for functional \textsc{RNA}s and proteins, and these molecules, in turn, determine cell function, structure, movement, transport, storage, defense, communication, and chemical reactions. Gene expression is the process by which information encoded in \textsc{DNA} is converted into these products, following the central dogma of molecular biology. For protein-coding genes, this process starts with transcription, where \textsc{DNA} serves as a template for \textsc{RNA} polymerase to produce messenger \textsc{RNA} (m\textsc{RNA}), followed by translation by ribosomes into a polypeptide chain that folds into a protein. For non-coding genes, the \textsc{RNA} products of transcription (small nuclear \textsc{RNA}s (sn\textsc{RNA}s), transfer \textsc{RNA}s (t\textsc{RNA}s), ribosomal \textsc{RNA}s (r\textsc{RNA}s), or microRNAs (mi\textsc{RNA}s)) themselves play roles in protein assembly, gene expression itself, and regulation. 

Transcription profiling provides a systematic approach for studying gene expression by quantifying the abundance of mRNA and non-coding transcripts in a biological sample under various conditions. Common profiling methods include microarrays \cite{lockhart2000genomics}, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) \cite{wang2009rna}, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) \cite{tang2009mrna}, and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) \cite{bustin2009miqe}, each with distinct characteristics, resolutions, and sensitivity. In this thesis, we conduct studies focusing on RNA-seq and scRNA-seq.

RNA-seq, also referred to as bulk RNA-seq, quantifies gene expression in a sample by aggregating mRNA abundance across all the cells by converting RNA into cDNA and sequencing it. It is an essential tool for comparing global changes and differences between, for example, healthy and diseased states. Nonetheless, it obscures heterogeneity when the sample contains multiple cell types or cells of the same type but at different stages of a biological process or under different conditions, each contributing differently to this overall averaged-out expression. For example, the tumor microenvironment is a heterogeneous mixture of malignant cells, stromal components, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, etc. In such contexts, measuring gene expression at the cell type- or even cell-level is extremely valuable and enables an array of new studies on cell-type identification, developmental trajectories, cell-cell communication, and rare cell identification \cite{tirosh2016dissecting, puram2017single}.

scRNA-seq, introduced in 2009, enhances the resolution of RNA-seq to the individual cells. At a high level, this is made possible by isolating individual cells and tagging mRNA from each individual cell with a unique barcode. Since then, new technologies have increased the throughput, sensitivity, and efficiency of experiments, and the number of profiled cells has reached hundreds of thousands or even millions in a single experiment on some platforms \cite{zheng2017massively}. Compared to bulk data, single-cell data usually suffers from high technical noise and sparsity \cite{hicks2018missing}.

One point to consider, which is sometimes overlooked in computational studies involving transcriptomics data, is that gene expression profiling is a good proxy for protein abundance but doesn’t directly translate to functional importance or protein levels due to many factors, including protein degradation and post-transcriptional modifications.

\subsection*{Mechanisms of Gene Regulation and Transcriptional Control}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Mechanisms of Gene Regulation and Transcriptional Control}
Interestingly, despite all cells of multicellular organisms containing the same static genome regardless of cell type and tissue of origin, they display vastly distinct characteristics, morphologies, and functions. This difference can primarily be attributed to differential gene expression resulting from precisely controlled regulation programs subject to spatio-temporal specificity. 

In this thesis, we mainly focus on eukaryotic organisms and transcriptional regulation. Gene regulation is controlled at different levels, which we present here at a high level:

\begin{itemize}
\item Epigenetic Regulation: DNA is wound around proteins called histones to form chromatin. When wound tightly (heterochromatin), it is more difficult for DNA to be transcribed, and easier when wound loosely (euchromatin). DNA methylation, histone modifications, chromatin remodeling, and non-coding RNAs that target chromatin can affect chromatin accessibility and thus, regulation. Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin Sequencing (ATAC-seq) \cite{buenrostro2013transposition} can identify open chromatin regions, and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-seq) \cite{johnson2007genome} can map binding sites of histone modifications or transcription factors (which we will introduce). These omics modalities are often used to study gene regulation.
\item Transcriptional Regulation: Transcription factors (TFs) are proteins that bind to specific DNA motifs and play significant roles in this regulatory stage. Eukaryotes have three RNA polymerases (Pol I, Pol II, and Pol III). Pol II is the instrument that creates the mRNA, but it cannot start transcription by itself. Its positioning at transcription start sites requires the assembly of a pre-initiation complex (PIC), which includes it as well as other proteins and basal (general) TFs (GTFs). GTFs guide Pol II to the promoter, a sequence of DNA upstream of the gene’s location,  close to the transcription start site \cite{thomas2006general}. Furthermore, sequence-specific TFs (around 1400-1600 of them \cite{lambert2018human}) that are not directly involved in PIC formation, characterized as activators that encourage or repressors that inhibit transcription, regulate transcription. They do so by recognizing and physically binding to specific motifs on cis-regulatory sequences such as enhancers and silencers. TF binding to enhancers promotes \textit{looping}, which brings the enhancer closer to the promoter of the target gene. When this happens, the mediator can send signals from the bound TF to Pol II and the PIC. Repressor TFs can block regulatory signals and the binding of other elements to the DNA, and even encourage chromatin inaccessibility through proteins such as histone deacetylases (HDACs).

\item Post-Transcriptional Regulation: At this level, the pre-mRNAs undergo processes such as capping to protect them from degradation, allowing their transport from the nucleus to the cytoplasm (only about \nicefrac{1}{20} make it out) and addition of a poly(A) tail to stabilize them and promote translation later on. Alternative splicing also determines what protein they can produce. 
\item Translational Regulation: Regulation at this stage involves many factors, such as the spatial location and upstream open reading frames (uORF) and motifs in the 5’ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of the mRNA, interactions with RNA-binding regulatory proteins (RBPs) and microRNAs (miRNAs). 
\item Post-Translational Regulation: The most common form of regulation occurs through modification of existing chemical functional groups, such as phosphates, acetates, carbohydrates, or lipids/hydrophobics, or their addition to specific amino acid side chains by covalent bonds. Such modifications change protein activity and structure, are mostly reversible, allowing high-speed regulation and reactions to stimuli.
\item Spatio-Temporal Regulation: Ensures that genes are expressed differently depending on environmental signals, subcellular location, and time, for example, during development and morphogenesis. It involves previously introduced regulatory mechanisms and other signaling pathways.

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Gene Regulatory Networks as System-Level Biological Models}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Gene Regulatory Networks as System-Level Biological Models}
Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) \cite{davidson2005gene} are directed graphs, where nodes represent genes and other regulatory elements such as TFs, proteins, miRNA, and signalling molecules, and edges depict their interactions that regulate gene expression. Edges can be weighted and signed by the strength and nature (inhibitory or inductive) of the interaction, which may also depend on external factors, including context, time, and location, other nodes, or interactions. They may contain cycles and self-regulating feedback loops. There are many simplified variations of  GRNs that are used to streamline computational modeling. Transcriptional regulatory networks (TRNs), for example, exclusively model transcriptional regulation by constraining the GRN to a directed acyclic bipartite graph between genes and TFs. Most computational studies in the field, including ours, involve TRNs, but refer to them as GRNs to align with common usage \cite{karamveer2024approaches}. 

GRNs are an end result, but an intermediate one in biomedical research. Ideally, they are used as causal maps of molecular interaction, allowing hypothesis generation, experimental design, and downstream analysis.  They can be used for comparative analysis across diseases and conditions as context-specific networks become more available \cite{emmert2014gene}. Importantly, as system-level networks, GRNs are a powerful tool for studying complex mechanisms that involve pathways rather than individual elements or genes. This is the case for many biological processes, such as cell differentiation, cancer progression, immune responses, and stimulus responses. Such understanding can aid applications such as precision medicine and drug design.

\section*{Gene Regulatory Network Inference and Benchmarking}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Gene Regulatory Network Inference and Benchmarking}
\subsection*{Computational \& Statistical Models of Gene Regulatory Network Inference}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Computational \& Statistical Models of Gene Regulatory Network Inference}

Data-driven GRN inference from bulk and later, from single-cell transcriptomics data has long been a focus in computational biology, leading to an extensive body of research. GRN inference methods can be classified along several dimensions, including their underlying methodology and the data modalities or auxiliary information they exploit. From a data-centric perspective, some methods rely exclusively on transcriptomic data, while others integrate additional sources of information, such as transcription factor binding motifs and motif enrichment analyses (SCENIC \cite{aibar2017scenic}), as well as epigenomic data, like ChIP-seq (AMuSR \cite{castro2019multi}) and ATAC-seq (Symphony \cite{burdziak2019nonparametric}, CellOracle \cite{kamimoto2023dissecting}). Such data is not always available, but it can lead to stronger inference.


From a methodological perspective, these methods broadly fall into four major classes: correlation-based (PPCOR \cite{kim2015ppcor}), information-theoretic (PIDC \cite{chan2017gene}, ARACNE \cite{margolin2006aracne}, C3NET \cite{altay2010inferring}), model-based (SCODE \cite{matsumoto2017scode}, GRISLI \cite{aubin2020gene}, SCNS \cite{woodhouse2018scns}), and machine learning-based (DeepSEM \cite{shu2021modeling}, DeepDRIM \cite{chen2021deepdrim}, GENIE3 \cite{huynh2010inferring}, GRNBoost2 \cite{moerman2019grnboost2}). Each methodology exhibits distinct strengths and limitations. Correlational methods cannot directly infer causal relationships, interaction directionality (as correlation is symmetrical), or distinguish between direct and indirect relationships. Information-theoretic approaches, typically based on mutual information or Bayesian conditional probability, can infer non-linear relations and incorporate pruning to filter out indirect relationships. However, they remain undirectional. Model-based approaches have explicit connections to biology through mechanistic modeling, which can be based on various models, such as ordinary differential equations (ODEs),  Bayesian networks (BNs), and probabilistic graphical models (PGMs). They often leverage trajectory and pseudotemporal data. Nevertheless, their assumptions about regulatory kinetics can be limiting. Finally, machine learning models make relatively few a priori assumptions and can infer higher-order non-linear relations, but they typically require large amounts of data. 

\subsection*{Experimental Validation-Based Benchmarks}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Experimental Validation-Based Benchmarks}
Assessing GRN inference methods remains a challenge due to the lack of a set of true edges, known as a gold standard, to make up ground truths corresponding to all the specific organisms, cell types, and conditions we intend to study. Ideally, we would establish a comprehensive causal ground truth through genetic knockdown, knockout, or overexpression perturbation experiments by conducting these experiments for all genes individually and their countless combinations. 

Although datasets obtained from regulator perturbation following sequencing exist and are used in studies, they are far from comprehensive due to the associated cost and time \cite{olsen2014inference, frohlich2008analyzing, mccalla2023identifying}. Furthermore, it is not possible to sequence the exact same cells twice (before and after perturbation), which complicates the establishment of direct causal relationships. More recent sequencing technologies, such as Live-seq \cite{chen2022live}, that preserve cell viability, can be used to overcome this issue. The KnockTF 2.0 database \cite{feng2024knocktf} contains expression profiles with knockdown or knockout of TF/transcription-cofactors for multiple species. ESCAPE \cite{xu2013escape} also includes data for differentially expressed genes following knockdown or overexpression. Recent high-throughput CRISPR-based perturbation screens, such as CROP-seq \cite{datlinger2017pooled} and Perturb-seq \cite{dixit2016perturb}, offer the potential to be used for benchmarking. 

Well-studied model organisms such as \textit{Escherichia coli} (E. coli) and \textit{Saccharomyces cerevisiae} (yeast) can be used to establish ground truths (DREAM5 challenges \cite{marbach2012wisdom}, RegulonDB \cite{salgado2024regulondb}). However, gene regulation in prokaryotic and unicellular eukaryotic organisms differs from that of higher eukaryotes, and insights from benchmarking might not translate to humans. Major studies often use ChIP-seq data to create ground truths. Although TF binding information is extremely valuable for studying regulation, it is context-dependent and does not alone directly establish regulatory relationships (or their kind: activation or inhibition),  or gene expression changes. As proteins are products of gene expression, it might seem reasonable to use protein-protein interaction (PPI) databases such as STRING \cite{mering2003string}. But, PPIs in STRING are at the organism level and undirectional. Moreover, proteins can interact for reasons unrelated to regulation.

Structured biological databases, such as TRRUST \cite{han2018trrust} (built using text mining), DoRothEA \cite{garcia2019benchmark}, CistromeDB \cite{zheng2019cistrome}, GTRD \cite{yevshin2019gtrd}, NGS-QC \cite{mendoza2013quality}, RegNetwork \cite{liu2015regnetwork}, and KEGG \cite{kanehisa2000kegg}, are exceptional resources, often with many interactions. Yet, many of these sources aggregate data across multiple experiments, organisms, or conditions, making them not context-specific, or rely on indirect evidence (such as PPI and ChIP-seq). On the other hand, curated networks (such as those used in BEELINE \cite{pratapa2020benchmarking}) can be accurate and context-specific, but are small.

\subsection*{Gene Regulatory-Guided Simulation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Gene Regulatory-Guided Simulation}
  GRN-guided simulators generate datasets given known user-provided ground truth regulatory interactions. They allow for quantitative and systematic evaluation of GRN inference under a tightly controlled setup. GeneNetWeaver (GNW) \cite{schaffter2011genenetweaver} is the earliest and possibly most influential example of such methods. It was originally developed for bulk RNA-seq and used for the community-driven benchmarks of the DREAM network inference challenges \cite{marbach2012wisdom}. GNW models both transcription and translation using a thermodynamic approach that allows for both additive and multiplicative regulatory interactions. To express the network in terms of Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs), each node $i$ in the network is assigned the rate of change of mRNA concentration and the rate of change of protein concentration as:  

\[\frac{{{\mathrm{d}}\left[ {x_{{i}}} \right]}}{{{\mathrm{d}t}}} = m\,f\left( {R_{{i}}} \right) - l_{{x}}\left[ {x_{{i}}} \right]\]

\[\frac{{{\mathrm{d}}\left[ {p_{{i}}} \right]}}{{{\mathrm{d}t}}} = r\left[ {x_{{i}}} \right] - l_{{p}}\left[ {p_{{i}}} \right]\]

where $m$ is the rate of mRNA transcription, $r$ is the translation rate, $R_i$ is the set of regulators of $i$, and $l_x$ and $l_p$ are the mRNA and protein degradation rates, respectively. $f$ models the relative activation (between 0 and 1) of node $i$ given the TF or protein concentration of its regulators $R_i$. To account for stochasticity in gene expression, GNW converts these ODEs into Chemical Langevin Equations (CLE) \cite{gillespie2000chemical}. It adds molecular noise proportional to the square root of the production and degradation rates terms to mimic thermal fluctuations:

\[
\frac{dX_t}{dt} = V(X_t) - D(X_t) + c\Big(\sqrt{V(X_t)}\,\eta_t + \sqrt{D(X_t)}\,\eta_d\Big)
\]

where $V(X_t)$ and $D(X_t)$ are the mRNA or protein production and degradation terms, respectively. $c$ regulates the intensity of the molecular noise and $\eta_v$ and $\eta_d$ are independent Gaussian white-noises. GNW uses the Stratonovich scheme and the Milstein method to integrate the two equations in the form of the Stochastic ODE (SDE) above and create an internal cell state \cite{schaffter2010numerical}.

Although GNW has been used in some single-cell studies, at times with minor adaptation (such as inducing dropout events to simulate sparsity) \cite{chan2017gene, chen2018evaluating}, it does not preserve trajectories or statistical properties of scRNA-seq \cite{chen2018evaluating}. Inspired by GNW, BoolODE \cite{pratapa2020benchmarking} was developed to extend it to handle Boolean regulatory logic by requiring the user to provide truth tables. When used in the BEELINE study \cite{pratapa2020benchmarking}, the best-performing GRN inference methods on its synthetic benchmarks performed poorly on non-synthetic benchmarks. Similarly, SERGIO \cite{dibaeinia2020sergio} uses SDEs, but follows a two-step approach by first generating a dataset strictly following the GRN, then relying on the user to match it to a reference by iteratively adjusting three parameters to add technical noise. This process not only burdens the user but introduces subjectivity (affecting reproducibility) and can also disrupt regulatory interactions imposed in the first simulation step. Importantly, neither of these methods preserves gene identities by default. Furthermore, to model cooperative regulation (multiple TFs regulating a gene), BoolODE relies on user-provided truth tables, and SERGIO assumes the combined effect of multiple TFs to be the sum of their individual effects. 

More recently, scMultiSim \cite{li2025scmultisim}, also following a mechanistic approach, uses a unified kinetic framework to generate multi-modal single-cell data (including gene expression, chromatin accessibility, RNA velocity, and spatial coordinates) while also modeling cell identity, GRNs, cell–cell interactions (CCI), and chromatin accessibility while incorporating technical noise. MOSim \cite{monzo2025mosim} allows for multilayer GRNs to be imposed, but does so by  “rearranging” a provided seed scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq to match defined regulatory patterns. 

\section*{Generative Modeling and In-Silico Single-Cell Transcriptomics}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Generative Modeling and In-Silico Single-Cell Transcriptomics}

Generative modeling is a branch of unsupervised learning that aims to learn an approximate probability distribution $p_{model}(x)$ from a finite set of observed data points $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(1)}, x^{(2)}, \dots, x^{(n)}\}$ drawn from an unknown true distribution $p_{data}(x)$. The main challenge in generative modeling is the high dimensionality of the same space. In single-cell transcriptomics, where the dimensionality corresponds to the number of genes, approximately 20000, most combinations of gene expression do not represent biologically realistic states. The manifold hypothesis offers a widely recognized theoretical perspective that makes generative modeling in high-dimensional settings possible \cite{fefferman2016testing}. It posits that many real-world datasets lie on intrinsically lower-dimensional geometric structures.


Once a generative model is learned, it can be used for three fundamental tasks: synthesis, density estimation, and representation learning. Synthesis is perhaps the most well-known capability of generative modeling with applications in image synthesis \cite{goodfellow2020generative}, text generation \cite{vaswani2017attention, radford2018improving}, and audio synthesis \cite{oord2016wavenet, wang2017tacotron}. It refers to the ability to draw “realistic” samples from the learned distribution samples that resemble observed data when $p_{model}(x)\approx p_{data}(x)$ without merely replicating them. Density estimation is the problem of explicitly or implicitly recovering the underlying probability density function of a dataset, allowing us to evaluate the likelihood of observations $x$ under the learned model. This application is at the core of unsupervised learning tasks such as anomaly detection \cite{chalapathy2018group, cao2016hybrid}. Since many generative models first encode the input data into meaningful latent spaces $z$ for reconstruction or generation back to a high-dimensional space ($p(x∣z)$, following the manifold hypothesis), their learned representations can be used for feature extraction, manifold learning, and downstream tasks such as dimensionality reduction and visualization, classification, and clustering \cite{bengio2013representation, kingma2013auto}.

\subsubsection*{Explicit Generative Models}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Explicit Generative Models}

Explicit density models, as the name implies, directly represent and learn a \textsc{pdf} $p_{model}(x; \theta)$, controlled by parameter $\theta$. Their objective is to maximize the likelihood of the training data by adjusting $\theta$ to make the observed data most probable under the model. Within this branch, models are further distinguished by the tractability of their density functions. 

\paragraph*{Tractable Explicit Density Models}
Models are classified as tractable when they allow for an exact evaluation and/or sampling of the likelihood at any given data point $x$ without requiring computationally expensive numerical methods or approximations. Generative models in this category include normalizing flow-based models (e.g., NICE \cite{dinh2014nice}, RealNVP \cite{dinh2016density}, Glow \cite{kingma2018glow}, MAF \cite{papamakarios2017masked}), autoregressive models (e.g., fully visible belief networks (NADE \cite{uria2016neural}, MADE \cite{germain2015made}, PixelRNN \cite{so2024pixelrnn}, PixelCNN \cite{van2016conditional}), WaveNet \cite{oord2016wavenet}, and GPT \cite{radford2018improving}), and change of variables models (e.g., nonlinear ICA \cite{hyvarinen1999nonlinear}).

Normalizing flow models, like many generative models, operate on the idea of transforming a simple base probability density $p_z(z)$, such as a multivariate Gaussian, into the complex target distribution $p_x(x)$ of the observed data through a series of invertible, differential mapping functions. To compute $p_x(x)$, these models apply the change of variables formula: 

\[
p_x(x) = p_z{z) \, \left| \det \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \right|
= p_z(f^{-1}(x)) \, \left| \det \frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial {x}} \right|,
\]
where $\det \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ is the Jacobian determinant. Computing determinants of large matrices is generally very slow ($O(n^3)$). Through architectural designs such as block-diagonal, triangular, additive, or affine coupling layers, flow-based models simplify the calculation of the Jacobian determinant to $O(n)$ \cite{dinh2014nice, dinh2016density, kingma2018glow}. 

Autoregressive models decompose a joint probability into a product of conditional probabilities, using the chain rule:

\[
p(x) = p(x_1, \dots, x_D) 
= \prod_{i=1}^{D} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}),
\] 

where $x_i$ is the $i$-th variable, and $D$ is the dimension of $x$ (a sample). Instead of generating an entire sample (e.g., an image or text) at once, the model generates units (e.g., pixels, words, or tokens) one at a time, conditioned on all units that preceded it, in a fixed, predefined ordering. As such, sampling cannot be readily parallelized, long-range context is hard to maintain, small errors can accumulate or “snowball”, making it difficult to maintain overall coherence over long sequences.   

While these models provide a clear principled means of training via direct likelihood optimization, they are often computationally expensive. Autoregressive models suffer from slow inference, while normalizing flow models are constrained by the requirement for invertible transformations with tractable Jacobians, which can hinder their performance on some complex data distributions.

\paragraph*{Approximate Explicit Density Models}
Oftentimes, the complexity of the target distribution makes the true density function mathematically intractable to compute or optimize directly, and models must rely on approximate density methods such as variational inference \cite{jordan1999introduction} or Markov chain-based sampling \cite{neal1993probabilistic}. Variational inference introduces latent variables $z$ and a parametric distribution $q_\phi(z|x)$ to approximate the true posterior $p(z|x)$. The objective becomes maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) on the log marginal likelihood: 
\[
\log p_\theta(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z)} \big[ \log p_\theta(x \mid z) \big] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z) \,\|\, p_\theta(z)\big)
\], 
where $KL$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. In variational autoencoders (VAEs) \cite{kingma2013auto}, the encoder and decoder neural networks parameterize \(q_\phi(z|x)\) and \(p_\theta(x|z)\), respectively. As VAE training involves stochastic gradient descent and directly sampling \(z \sim q_\phi(z|x)\) would block backpropagation, the “reparameterization trick”  is used to express \(z\) as a differentiable function of the encoder outputs $\mu_\phi(x)$ and $\sigma_\phi(x)$ for each input $x$ and random noise: 
\[
z = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \cdot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\]

Another class of models in this category is Markov-chain-based models, where the probability density is specified through an energy function. Sampling is done by modeling generation as a sequence of stochastic state transitions that converges to the desired distribution \cite{neal1993probabilistic, geman1984stochastic}. Typical examples of energy-based methods (EMBs) include Boltzmann machines \cite{hinton1984boltzmann} and their variations. They define a join distribution over visible units $v$ (representing observed data) and hidden units $h$ (that capture underlying features): 

\[
p(v,h;\theta) = \frac{\exp\left(-E(v,h;\theta)\right)}{Z(\theta)}
\]

\[
Z(\theta) = \sum_{v,h} \exp\left(-E(v,h;\theta)\right),
\]


where $E(v,h;\theta)$ is the energy function and $Z(\theta)$ is the partition function. $p(v,h;\theta)$ specifies the likeliness of particular combinations of hidden and visible states, as determined by the overall energy associated with that configuration. Computing $Z(\theta)$ requires summing over all possible configurations, making its computation intractable. We overcome this issue by relying on approximate Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods such as Contrastive Divergence \cite{hinton2002training} and Gibbs sampling \cite{geman1984stochastic}. Learning can be expressed as the difference between the expectation under the data distribution (positive phase) and the expectation under the model distribution (negative phase), approximated by running a short Markov chain initialized at the data.
\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_{ij}} = \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{data}} - \langle v_i h_j \rangle_{\text{model}}
\]
 Models like Deep Boltzmann Machines \cite{salakhutdinov2009deep} or Deep Belief Networks \cite{hinton2006fast} (composed of stacks of Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs) \cite{smolensky1986information}) use a similar concept for parameter learning and have explicit but approximate density. 


\subsubsection*{Implicit Generative Models}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Implicit Generative Models}

Implicit generative models do not explicitly define a density function for the data. The goal is to sample realistic data points from a model distribution that is implicitly learned by the process used to generate the data itself, without relying on likelihood maximization. As a result, these models can only rely on indirect objectives such as adversarial and reconstruction losses or learned stochastic transitions.  Despite not being capable of density estimation, they synthesize very realistic data and empirically outperform explicit approximate density models such as VAEs \cite{kingma2013auto}. 

\paragraph*{Direct Implicit Density Models}
These models learn a direct mapping from a simple latent distribution (e.g., multivariate Gaussian noise) to the training data distribution (that is usually high-dimensional and complex) in a single pass without the need to sample iteratively. Generative Adversarial Networks \cite{goodfellow2020generative}, which we will discuss later on, are a classic example. 


\paragraph*{Markov Chain Implicit Density Models}
The sampling process in these models requires iteratively applying transitions until convergence by learning the stochastic transition operator of a Markov chain whose stationary distribution represents the target distribution. An emblematic example is Generative Stochastic Networks (GSNs) \cite{alain2016gsns}, which generalize Denoising Auto-Encoders (DAE) \cite{vincent2010stacked}.  GSNs learn the data manifold by being injected with noise and attempting to reconstruct the original data. They are trained at each step to predict a conditional distribution $P_\theta\!\left(X_{t+1} \mid H_t\right)$, where $X_{t+1}$ is the next reconstructed sample and $H_t$ is the hidden state at the current step of the Markov chain. Learning these local transitions is generally more simple than the entire data distribution. Because the Markov chain steps through small, local moves (as noise is injected at each step), its transition distributions usually include only a limited set of dominant modes near the current state and effectively becomes unimodal as moves become small. Consequently, GSNs' learning objectives resemble supervised function approximation rather than unsupervised global density estimation, and avoid computing intractable partition functions, unlike Boltzmann machines \cite{hinton1984boltzmann}.

\subsubsection*{Hybrid Generative Models}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Hybrid Generative Models}
This branch of the taxonomy includes models that incorporate elements from both explicit and implicit generative modeling. Diffusion models \cite{ho2020denoising}, for example, follow a fully fixed and tractable stochastic forward process that gradually adds noise to the data, forming an explicit likelihood-based model of data corruption. While diffusion models share the denoising mechanisms of GSNs \cite{alain2016gsns}, they are not implicit. GSNs use denoising to learn a transition operator for sampling. Meanwhile, in diffusion models, stepwise denoising serves as a proxy for score matching, where the model learns the gradient of the log-density of the data ($\nabla_x \log p(x)$ \cite{song2019generative}. This allows diffusion models to be trained using likelihood-based objectives like ELBO, similar to VAEs \cite{kingma2013auto}. However, they differ from explicit models as their reverse process is intractable and learned implicitly through neural network approximations.

\subsection*{Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)}
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \cite{goodfellow2014generative} are implicit generative models that learn a differentiable mapping function $G$, from a simple latent distribution (e.g., Gaussian or uniform noise) to the training distribution ($x_{gen} = G(z; \theta_g)$). To learn the function $G$, GANs follow a game-theoretic approach, using two players implemented as neural networks. A generator ($G$) with the goal of generating “fake” samples that closely mimic “real” samples (from the reference data) to deceive the second player. The discriminator ($D$) takes in samples from both the generator and the reference dataset, and tries to distinguish between them by assigning a probability that the input is real rather than fake. GANs are trained using stochastic gradient-based optimization methods, typically variants of the Adam optimizer, which update the parameters of both the generator and discriminator iteratively. During each update, one network’s parameters are temporarily “frozen” while the other is optimized. GAN training involes the value function $V(D, G)$:
\[\min_{G} \max_{D} V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{data}(x)}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_{z}(z)}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]\]

The generator aims to minimize this objective while the discriminator seeks to maximize it. As the adversarial training approach is a zero-sum game (one player’s gain is equivalent to the other player’s loss), during training, the generator will update its parameters to maximize the probability of the discriminator misclassifying its outputs, while the discriminator, in turn, will update its parameters to better tell apart real and generated samples. By symbiotically providing feedback during training, the two adversaries can co-evolve together. The Nash equilibrium is reached when the generator outputs become indistinguishable from real data, and the discriminator outputs a probability of 0.5 for all inputs.

\subsection*{On the Convergence and Mode Collapse of GANs}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{On Convergence and Mode Collapse of GANs}
GAN training, at least in the vanilla formulation introduced \cite{goodfellow2014generative}, is infamously fickle and unstable, and vulnerable to numerous failure modes \cite{salimans2016improved}. Mode collapse is one of the most common issues faced, where the generator learns to primarily produce from a subset of the reference data distribution, ignoring other modes. Intuitively, this issue arises when the generator has little incentive to generate from other modes of the data, when the discriminator consistently underperforms in its task. The adversarial training procedure must follow a delicate balance between the two players. Achieving this balance is a challenge, as GAN training is designed to follow the optimization procedure of a minimax game, which is not equivalent to that of the maximin game ($\min\limits_{G} \max\limits_{D} V(D, G) \neq \max\limits_{D} \min\limits_{G} V(D, G)$). The theoretical guarantees of GANs rely on solving the minimax game, but in practice, it is not possible to achieve the optimal discriminator in a single step. Instead, training alternates between $G$ and $D$, which can result in a drift toward a maximin-like regime, where the generator gets optimized against a suboptimal, fixed discriminator. A simple solution is to perform multiple updates of the discriminator for every update of the generator so that the generator is always provided with meaningful gradients. Unrolled GANs \cite{metz2016unrolled} are another solution that mitigates this problem by “looking ahead” at future discriminator updates during the generator optimization. 

GANs implicitly minimize the Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence between $p_{model}(x)$ and $p_{data}(x)$, provided $D$ is optimal. When the supports of these two distributions in low-dimensional manifolds are non-overlapping, the JS divergence saturates at a constant value of $\log(2)$. Consequently, training becomes unstable and the gradient of the loss function with respect to the generator's parameters vanishes, providing no feedback for the generator to evolve. To address this issue, Wasserstein GANs (WGANs) \cite{arjovsky2017wasserstein} replace JS divergence with the Wasserstein-1 (Earth Mover’s) distance:

\[
W(p_{\text{data}}, p_g) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(p_{\text{data}}, p_g)} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \gamma} \left[ \| x - y \| \right],
\]

where $\Pi(p_{\text{data}}, p_g)$ are the set of all possible "transport plans" (joint distributions) that have marginals $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$. Intuitively, this distance measures the minimum cost of transporting mass from one distribution to the other and provides meaningful gradients even when the two distributions are disjoint in the low-dimensional manifold. The above equation is computationally intractable. For GAN training, it is rewritten using the Kantorovich-Rubinstein Duality as:

\[
W(p_{\text{data}}, p_g) = \sup_{\| f \|_L \leq 1} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_g}[f(x)]
\]

Here, our critic (which replaces the discriminator as it outputs real-valued score instead of probabilities) approximates the function $f$ and must be $1$-Lipschitz continuous ($|f(x) - f(y)| \le |x - y|$).

Early WGANs enforced this constraint by weight clipping, which limited model capacity. A more optimal approach involves penalizing the WGANs if the gradient norm of the Critic deviates from 1 by adding a gradient penalty term \cite{gulrajani2017improved} to the WGAN objective that is regulated by $\lambda$.

\[
\mathcal{L}_D = 
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{x} \sim p_g}[D(\tilde{x})] - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{real}}[D(x)]}_{\text{Wasserstein distance}}
+ 
\underbrace{\lambda \, \mathbb{E}_{\hat{x} \sim p_{\hat{x}}} \left[ \left(\|\nabla_{\hat{x}} D(\hat{x})\|_2 - 1\right)^2 \right],}_{\text{Gradient Penalty}}
\]

where $\hat{x} = \epsilon x + (1-\epsilon)\tilde{x}$ is a random sample interpolated between real and generated samples. Wasserstein GANs with gradient penalty (WGAN-GPs) have shown to be successful in tackling model collapse and improving convergence and training stability across data modalities.  




\subsection*{Conditional and Controllable Generation with GANs}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Conditional and Controllable Generation with GANs}
Conditional GANs (cGANs) \cite{mirza2014conditional} offer a method for directly generating samples from user-specified categorical labels $y$. The most direct form of conditioning involves concatenating one-hot-encoded class labels to the input of both the generator and the discriminator. In this setup, the generator produces $G(z|y)$, and the discriminator evaluates $D(x|y)$, generating samples that are not only realistic but also adhere to the provided conditions. Over the years, implementations of cGANs have been extended beyond discrete labels to continuous variables (InfoGAN \cite{chen2016infogan}), structured attributes (AC-GAN \cite{odena2017conditional}), textual embeddings (stackedGANs \cite{zhang2018stackgan++}), feature maps (Pix2Pix \cite{isola2017image}, CycleGAN \cite{zhu2017unpaired}), and more. Furthermore, numerous new conditioning techniques have been proposed that improve class fidelity and training stability. For example, auxiliary classifier GANs (AC-GANs \cite{odena2017conditional}) add an additional classification head to the discriminator that predicts the conditioning label for both real and generated samples. In projection-based conditioning cite{miyato2018cgans}, the discriminator takes the inner product between learned class embeddings ($V$) and intermediate feature representations ($\phi(x)$) by projecting the one-hot label ($y$), and adds this term to its output score (resulting in $D(x, y) =  y^TV\phi(x) + \psi(\phi(x))$). Conditioning can also be integrated in the generator using conditional batch normalization (CBN) \cite{de2017modulating}. In CBN, the scale ($\gamma(y)$) and shift ($\beta(y)$) parameters of normalization layers are made functions of the conditioning variable ($y$), modulating hidden activations ($h$): $\text{CBN}(h, y) = \gamma(y) \hat{h} + \beta(y)$.

A complementary line of work to cGANs is controllable generation, which offers more fine-grained control over synthesized outputs without the need for labeled samples by interpreting and deliberately sampling the latent space. These efforts were greatly influenced by the ability to interpolate the latent space and produce smooth semantic transitions on images of faces (e.g., from young to old) and perform arithmetic operations in the $Z$-space (e.g., $Z_{\text{smiling man}} = Z_{\text{smiling woman}} - Z_{\text{neutral woman}} + Z_{\text{neutral man}}$ produces a smiling man) in DCGAN \cite{radford2015unsupervised}. Yet, GAN latent spaces are entangled, and features are correlated, making the manipulation of an attribute affect other ones (we will discuss how to overcome this problem with CausalGANs \cite{kocaoglu2017causalgan}). Substantial efforts have been made to achieve GAN latent space disentanglement. InfoGAN encourages disentanglement by introducing a mutual information regularization term to ensure that specific latent codes are strongly predictive of interpretable features in the generated data. Other methods penalize statistical dependence between latent dimensions \cite{yu2021michigan}. StyleGAN \cite{karras2019style} introduces a generator architecture with intermediate latent spaces to separate high-level semantics from stochastic variations at multiple levels of hierarchy.

\subsection*{CausalGAN}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{CausalGAN}
%Generative modeling
%Taxonomy of generative models
% GANs
%	Latent space interpolation
% 	Mode Collapse
%	WGAN-GPs
%	WGAN-GP for single-cell
%	Conditional GANs (cscGAN)
Conditional GAN architectures generate samples from class-conditional data distributions by conditioning on class labels that are modeled independently of eachother. A conditional GAN trained on images of faces, given labels \textit{mustache} and \textit{sex}, will simply sample from a subset of its learned training distribution (corresponding to $sex = male$) when conditioned on $mustache = true$. However, since the choice of one label (here, \textit{mustache}) does not impact other labels (\textit{sex}), we expect the model to output samples corresponding to both male and female labels. CausalGAN \cite{kocaoglu2017causalgan} captures interdependencies between labels themselves (e.g., \textit{sex causes mustache} ($S \rightarrow M$)) through a causal graph (Figure \ref{fig:causalgraph}) and the causal effect between labels and the output by conditioning on labels (i.e., \textit{labels cause output} ($L \rightarrow O$)). By explicitly modeling the data-generating process as a causal mechanism, CausalGAN can not only sample from observational and conditional distributions but also interventional ones to synthesize samples that do not exist in the training dataset. Intervening on a label, for example, forcing $mustache = true$, only affects its descendants in the causal graph (e.g., \textit{output}). This allows the model to generate both male and female faces with mustaches because the intervention breaks the causal link to the parent node (\textit{sex}). In contrast, conditioning allows information to flow upstream to parent labels. By conditioning on mustache, we inadvertently update the probability of the \textit{sex} label, shifting it heavily towards male.

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.67\textwidth]{figures/causalgraph.png}
  \caption[Example of a possible causal graph]{Example of a possible causal graph.}
  \label{fig:causalgraph}
\end{figure}


At the core of CausalGAN’s architecture is a generator that strictly adheres to a user-provided causal graph (limited to a DAG), inheriting its neural connections directly from that causal structure. Thus, each variable is generated as a function of its parents in the DAG and independent noise. Returning to our example, under the causal sufficiency assumption, meaning there are no unobserved common causes (confounders) affecting the labels, the generation process for each node follows the path of the arrows:
\begin{itemize}
\item $N_S \rightarrow Sex$: represented as $Sex = f_S(N_S)$
\item $\{Sex, N_M\} \rightarrow Mustache$: represented as $Mustache = f_M(Sex, N_M)$
\item $\{Sex, Mustache, N_O\} \rightarrow Output$: represented as $Output = f_O(Sex, Mustache, N_O)$
\end{itemize}
where $f_S$, $f_M$, and $f_O$ are functions that can represented by feedforward neural networks and the exogenous noise terms $N_S$, $N_M$, and $N_O$ are jointly independent. In practice, simultaneously training such a generator to learn a joint distribution over discrete labels and high-dimensional images is challenging. CausalGAN employs a modular two-step approach. 
\begin{enumerate}
\item Causal Controller (Label Generator): A causal implicit model is trained on all causal graph nodes (labels) preceding the output, using the DAG to arrange the generator’s structure to generate labels in their topological order. Standard GAN training with Jensen-Shannon divergence has difficulties sampling from binary distributions; CausalGAN utilizes a WGAN-GP, which they empirically show can be used to output \textit{essentially discrete} (sharply concentrated at 0 and 1) labels. Once trained, the causal controller is frozen and no longer updated.
\item Conditional Image Generator: This stage focuses on transitioning from all labels to the final output ($L \rightarrow O$). An image generator is trained conditioned on the outputs of the causal controller. Integrated with the pretrained causal controller,  it forms a causal implicit generative model over both labels and images. 
\end{enumerate}

\begin{figure}[h!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/causalgan.png}
  \caption[The CausalGAN Architecture]{The CausalGAN Architecture (adapted from CausalGAN \cite{kocaoglu2017causalgan}).}
  \label{fig:causalgan}
\end{figure}


In addition to the generator ($G$) and discriminator ($D$), CausalGAN extends the standard adversarial framework by incorporating two auxiliary networks: a Labeler and an Anti-Labeler (Figure \ref{fig:causalgan}). The Labeler is trained on real and generated images, tasked with recovering labels used to produce them. By minimizing the Labeler loss, we encourage the conditional image generator to capture labels. Conversely, the Anti-Labeler is trained only on synthetic samples with the same task. Counterintuitively, the Anti-Labeler loss is maximized and serves as a critical regularizer in CausalGAN. If the generator were to generate repeated samples for specific label combinations, the Anti-Labeler would easily achieve high accuracy in the early stages of training. By maximizing this loss, the generator is effectively penalized for label-conditioned model collapse, encouraging it to produce more diverse samples. As training progresses, maximizing Anti-Labeler loss and minimizing Labeler loss become conflicting objectives. CausalGAN exponentially decays the weight of the Anti-Labeler loss as training progresses.


Under mild assumptions, notably that the joint distribution over labels is strictly positive, theoretical guarantees for CausalGAN establish that the two-stage procedure produces a valid causal implicit generative model, where the generator is proven to match the true class conditional output distribution ($p_{data}(x|l)$) at its global minimum. While this guarantee is for single binary labels, it can extend to multiple binary labels using cross-entropy losses for each label. This setup primarily captures individual label posteriors $p_r(l_i|x) = p_g(l_i|x)$, where $p_r(l_i|x)$ is the probability of the $i$-th label being present in a real image and $p_g(l_i|x)$ is the same for a fake image. However, it is sufficient to recover the full joint label posterior given the image in cases where the labels are completely determined by the image (as is the case for the CelebA dataset \cite{liu2015deep} used in the CausalGAN study).


\subsection*{In-Silico Simulation of scRNA-seq}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{In-Silico Simulation of scRNA-seq}
The expansion in availability and access to large-scale single-cell datasets has driven the rapid development of computational methods for their analysis and interpretation. As a result, in-silico simulation has become essential for evaluating their performance with known ground truths, which are often unavailable or infeasible to obtain experimentally. Simulation allows for assessing methods for scalability and robustness and under a range of tightly controlled, manipulable experimental scenarios (controlling for gene-gene correlation, relative abundance of cell types, and technical variations, such as batch effects, doublet rates, dropout, and sequencing depths, amongst others). Numerous studies have employed single-cell simulators for benchmarking methods for differential expression analysis, dimensionality reduction, multi-omics integration, imputation, batch effect removal, doublet detection, trajectory inference, clustering, marker detection, biological network inference, and cell type annotation \cite{cao2021benchmark, crowell2023shaky}. 

In addition, simulators can be used for data augmentation, generating synthetic observations to be used alongside real ones, leading to a boost in performance, robustness, and higher reproducibility rates \cite{marouf2020realistic}. An interesting yet underexplored and understandably controversial application of single-cell simulators is their potential use in privacy-preserving data sharing. While synthetic data generation offers the promise of masking sensitive, proprietary, or patient information, recent work has shown that these approaches can be vulnerable to adversarial privacy attacks \cite{golob2026privacy}. 

Single-cell simulators can be classified in numerous ways; however, a broad distinction can be made between de novo simulators, which generate data from scratch, and reference-based simulators, which rely on real datasets to guide the simulation. Do novo simulation (e.g., BoolODE \cite{pratapa2020benchmarking} and GNW \cite{schaffter2011genenetweaver}) is especially useful for creating a wide range of hypothetical or theoretical scenarios without being constrained by a particular reference. These approaches offer unmatched flexibility in controlling a multitude of specific biological signals. Naturally, this flexibility comes with a trade-off: users often must have a solid understanding of the simulator’s underlying process to make informed decisions about simulation parameters, which can be burdensome and carry the risk of generating unrealistic datasets if their choices do not reflect biologically plausible conditions. It can also be challenging to validate their biological faithfulness or trust that studies on the resulting datasets will be transferable to real data \cite{crowell2023shaky}.  

Reference-based models mimic the statistical properties and gene expression profiles of a given dataset. They generally require less user input and produce realistic data by design, which can be validated for realism. However, they can be limited by the complexity of their reference. As more data becomes available, general-purpose simulation for augmenting existing data will become less needed, and these simulators would have to go beyond reproducing what already exists. As with many method classifications, there are hybrid options that combine elements from both to balance the trade-off between flexibility and realism. They typically involve a two-step process, either estimating parameters from a reference before adding de novo features post-hoc or generating de novo effects and subsequently aligning them with reference data, as in the case of SERGIO \cite{dibaeinia2020sergio}.

Beyond this classification, simulation methods differ in their modeling assumptions, which range from fully parametric to semi-parametric or non-parametric. Underlying the majority of well-known parametric simulators is a set of statistical frameworks for count modeling. Historically, the negative binomial (NB) has been the default choice for discrete count modeling of bulk data (BASiCS \cite{vallejos2015basics}, hierarchicell \cite{zimmerman2021hierarchicell}, muscat \cite{crowell2020muscat}, and powsimR \cite{vieth2017powsimr} all use NB for single-cell) as it accounts for overdispersion through a gene-specific dispersion parameter. To account for the sparsity in single-cell data, its variation, the zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB used in ZINB-WaVE \cite{risso2018general}, and zingeR \cite{van2017zinger}), has become a popular choice in this domain. Other approaches that offer more flexibility include the gamma-Poisson (ESCO \cite{tian2021esco}, splat/splatter \cite{zappia2017splatter}), zero-inflated log-normal Poisson mixtures (POWSC \cite{su2020simulation}), Bayesian NB mixture (scDD \cite{korthauer2016statistical}), gamma-normal mixture (scDesign \cite{li2019statistical}), beta-Poisson (SymSim \cite{zhang2019simulating}, SCRIP \cite{qin2022scrip}), gamma-multivariate hypergeometric (SPARSim \cite{baruzzo2020sparsim}), and zero-inflated Poisson or NB with a Gaussian copula for gene-gene correlations (scDesign2 \cite{sun2021scdesign2}). Deep learning models such as scVI \cite{lopez2018deep} and scGAN \cite{marouf2020realistic} break free from making strong distributional assumptions about the data, while semi-parametric approaches like SPsimSeq \cite{assefa2020spsimseq} do not require the same large source datasets and provide a flexible compromise between the two.


\subsubsection*{GANs Applied to scRNA-seq}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{In-Silico Simulation of scRNA-seq}
Numerous GAN-based methods were developed to tackle challenges in the scRNA-seq domain, including data augmentation \cite{ghahramani2018generative, marouf2020realistic, lall2022lsh, ganguly2025graph}, dropout imputation \cite{xu2020scigans, huang2023scggan, wang2023scmultigan}, batch effect correction \cite{guan2024awgan,wang2022respan}, and cross-modality translation \cite{amodio2022single, xu2024scact}. Ghahramani et al. \cite{ghahramani2018generative} were one of the first to apply GANs to scRNA-seq. They found that WGAN-GPs lead to better training stability than vanilla GANs. This study also examined the use of learned features for dimensionality reduction and demonstrated a case of controllable generation by interpolating in the latent space (between $z_{\text{basal}$ and $z_{\text{differentiated}}$) to predict intermediate gene expression profiles of epidermal differentiation. 

However, applied to ~20000 genes, realism was subpar, and synthetic samples failed to capture the entire diversity of samples, even when qualitatively evaluated on a 2-dimensional t-SNE embedding. scRNA-seq data are highly heterogeneous with great cell-to-cell variability, even for the same cell types and populations. By library-size normalizing input cells, scGAN \cite{marouf2020realistic} saw a significant improvement in simulation quality, likely due to gradient stabilization resulting from gene expression being similarly ranged across cells. Furthermore, scGAN introduced a library-size normalization layer (LSN) to explicitly output the same read count as the training data for all generated cells, leaving the GAN with one less feature to learn. The same study also introduced cscGAN \cite{marouf2020realistic}, a projection-based conditional \cite{miyato2018cgans} extension of scGAN with CBN \cite{de2017modulating} layers for conditioning on labels such as cell type, state, and cluster. Using cscGAN for data augmentation led to improvements in downstream classification and trajectory analysis tasks. Other augmentation methods include LSH-GAN \cite{lall2022lsh}, which guides the generator using locality-sensitive hashing to focus training on informative subsets of real samples, and GARAGE \cite{ganguly2025graph}, which augments the generator’s input with attention-weighted embeddings of real cells derived from a k-nearest-neighbor graph via a graph attention network. 

In scRNA-seq, dropouts are zero-valued gene expression measurements due to technical limitations, such as low mRNA capture. Imputing these missing values is a challenge, as scRNA-seq data is sparse and standard methods that rely on borrowing information from similar cells risk oversmoothing and removing biological noise. scIGAN \cite{xu2020scigans} uses generated cells rather than observed cells to avoid these problems. scGGAN \cite{huang2023scggan} learns gene-gene relations using Graph Convolutional Networks \cite{kipf2016semi} (GCN)-based GAN, which uses a learned gene-gene network to simulate data used for imputation. scMultiGAN \cite{wang2023scmultigan} also does imputation through first training two generators, one to simulate authentic gene expression (without dropout) and another to model dropout. Then, a third generator uses pre-trained knowledge from the first training step to input raw expression and output imputed values. All the models discussed for imputation first reshape scRNA-seq data into an image-like format for training.

GANs are also used to remove batch effects (non-biological differences between datasets from different experiments). For example, AWGAN \cite{guan2024awgan} uses attention-guided WGANs, while ResPAN \cite{wang2022respan} employs WGAN-GPs along with random walk mutual nearest neighbor pairing and fully skip-connected autoencoders to map cells from a query batch to a reference batch. Methods such as scMMGAN \cite{amodio2022single} and scACT \cite{xu2024scact} use cycle-consistency, inspired by CycleGAN \cite{zhu2017unpaired}, to integrate or map measurements across modalities, including scRNA-seq.
